\section{Asymptotic Notations}

\subsection*{Definitions and Properties}

When analyzing an algorithm, we care more about how its running time grows with
input size than about its exact number of operations. Asymptotic notation
formalizes this idea by describing growth for sufficiently large inputs while
ignoring constant factors.

\begin{definition}[Big-O]
Let $f = f(n)$ and $g=g(n)$ be two functions that are positive for all integers $n$.
We say $f = O(g)$ if there exists a positive number $c > 0$ and an integer $N \ge 0$
such that $f(n) \le c\cdot g(n)$ for all $n\ge N$.
\end{definition}

Big-O gives an upper bound for sufficiently large input sizes, with constant factors ignored.
%Think of $c\cdot g(n)$ as an upper bound that $f(n)$ stays below once $n$ reaches the threshold $N$.
The constant $c$ allows us to scale $g(n)$ by a fixed factor ``for free'' when bounding $f(n)$ from above,
and the threshold $N$ lets us ignore behavior on small inputs.
Once chosen, $c$ and $N$ must work for \emph{every} $n\ge N$;
we cannot choose a new $c$ for different $n$.
Intuitively, Big-O is analogous to ``$\le$''.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(n)=3n^2+5n+2$ and $g(n)=n^2$. To prove $f=O(g)$ using the definition,
we need just one pair of constants $c$ and $N$ for which we can show that $f(n) \le c\cdot g(n)$ when $n\ge N$.
What are good choices? To bound $f(n)$ above by $c\cdot g(n)$, we need $c > 3$.
We could pick $c = 4$. Then $f(n) - c\cdot g(n) = 3n^2+5n+2 -4n^2 = 5n+2-n^2$.
To make it non-positive, i.e., $5n+2-n^2 = (5-n)\cdot n + 2\le 0$ for every $n\ge N$,
it suffices to pick $N = 6$: for $n\ge 6$, we have $(5-n)n+2\le -n+2\le -4$.
Thus $c=4$ and $N=6$ satisfy the definition. There is no need to find the
smallest possible $c$ or $N$: any pair that makes the inequality hold suffices.

%It need not be a tight bound: $n=O(n^2)$ is true even though $n$ grows much
%more slowly than $n^2$.

Some algorithms have more than one input-size parameter; for example,
the algorithm for merging two sorted arrays in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}
has the array lengths $m$ and $n$ as parameters.
We extend the definition to functions of multiple variables.

\begin{definition}[Big-O]
Let $f = f(m,n)$ and $g=g(m,n)$ be two functions that are positive on integers $m$ and $n$.
We say $f = O(g)$ if there exists a positive number $c > 0$ and integers $M\ge 0$ and $N \ge 0$
such that $f(m,n) \le c\cdot g(m,n)$ for all $m\ge M$ and $n\ge N$.
\end{definition}

%Here, the same constant $c$ must work for every pair $(m,n)$ with $m\ge M$ and $n\ge N$.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(m,n) = 4m + 4n + 5$ and $g(m,n) = m + n$. We now show that $f = O(g)$,
using the above definition. To show it, we need to find $c$, $M$, and $N$.
There are many choices, and one of them is $c = 7$, $M = 1$, and $N = 1$.
Let's verify: $f(m,n) - c\cdot g(m,n) = 4m + 4n + 5 - 7m - 7n = 5 - 3m - 3n \le 5 - 3 - 3 = -1 \le 0$,
where we use that $m \ge M = 1$ and $n \ge N = 1$. This proves that $f = O(g)$.

\begin{definition}[Big-Omega]
Let $f = f(n)$ and $g=g(n)$ be two functions that are positive for all integers $n$.
We say $f = \Omega(g)$ if there exists a positive number $c > 0$ and an integer $N \ge 0$
such that $f(n) \ge c\cdot g(n)$ for all $n\ge N$.
\end{definition}

Big-Omega gives a \emph{lower bound} for sufficiently large input sizes, with constant factors ignored.
%after some threshold, $f(n)$ stays above the floor $c\cdot g(n)$.
Big-Omega is analogous to ``$\ge$''.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(n) = 3n^2+5n+2$ and $g(n) = n^2$.
We have $f = \Omega(g)$, because we can pick $c = 1$ and $N =1$ and verify that
$f(n) - c\cdot g(n) = 3n^2+5n+2- n^2 = 2n^2 + 5n+2\ge 0$ for all $n\ge N$.

Similarly, we can define Big-Omega for multiple-variable functions.

\begin{definition}[Big-Omega]
Let $f = f(m,n)$ and $g=g(m,n)$ be two functions that are positive on integers $m$ and $n$.
We say $f = \Omega(g)$ if there exists a positive number $c > 0$ and integers $M\ge 0$ and $N \ge 0$
such that $f(m,n) \ge c\cdot g(m,n)$ for all $m\ge M$ and $n\ge N$.
\end{definition}

%As with multivariable Big-O, this lower bound must hold uniformly: one
%constant $c$ works for every pair beyond the two thresholds, regardless of
%the relative sizes of $m$ and $n$.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(m,n) = 4m + 4n + 5$ and $g(m,n) = m + n$. We now show that $f = \Omega(g)$.
%using the above definition. To show it, we need to find $c$, $M$, and $N$.
We can choose $c = 1$, $M = 0$, and $N = 0$.
Verify: $f(m,n) - c\cdot g(m,n) = 4m + 4n + 5 - m - n = 5 + 3m + 3n \ge 5 \ge 0$,
where $m \ge M = 0$ and $n \ge N = 0$.

The following claim follows directly from the definitions.
\begin{claim}
$f = O(g)$ if and only if $g = \Omega(f)$.
\end{claim}
\emph{Proof.} We have
		\begin{displaymath}
		\begin{array}{llll}
		& & f = O(g) \\
		& \Leftrightarrow & \exists\ c > 0, N \ge 0, \textrm{ s.t.\ } f(n) \le c\cdot g(n), \forall n \ge N\\
		& \Leftrightarrow & \exists\ c > 0, N \ge 0, \textrm{ s.t.\ } 1/c \cdot f(n) \le g(n), \forall n \ge N\\
		& \Leftrightarrow & \exists\ c' = 1/c > 0, N \ge 0, \textrm{ s.t.\ } g(n) \ge c'\cdot f(n), \forall n \ge N\\
		& \Leftrightarrow & g = \Omega(f) 
		\end{array}
		\end{displaymath} \qed

\begin{definition}[Big-Theta]
We say $f = \Theta(g)$ if and only if $f = O(g)$ and $f = \Omega(g)$.
\end{definition}

Big-Theta gives a \emph{tight bound}: $f$ grows at the same rate as $g$,
up to constant factors and for sufficiently large input sizes.
Big-Theta is analogous to ``$=$''.

%For a single variable, this means that there are
%constants $c_1,c_2>0$ and a common threshold $N$ such that
%\[
%    c_1 \cdot g(n) \le f(n) \le c_2 \cdot g(n) \qquad\text{for all } n\ge N.
%\]
%
%The upper and lower bounds may use different constants. If their thresholds
%differ, take the larger threshold so that both bounds hold at once. For two
%variables, take the larger threshold for each variable.

\emph{Example.}
$3n^2 + 5n + 2 = \Theta(n^2)$ as we proved both Big-O and Big-Omega between them.
For the same reason, $4m + 4n + 5 = \Theta(m + n)$.



\begin{definition}[small-o]
Let $f = f(n)$ and $g=g(n)$ be two functions that are positive for all integers $n$.
We say $f = o(g)$ if for every $c > 0$ there exists an integer $N_c \ge 0$, where $N_c$ may depend on $c$,
such that $f(n) \le c\cdot g(n)$ for all $n\ge N_c$.
%Let $f = f(n)$ and $g = g(n)$ be two positive functions. We say $f = o(g)$ if and only if $\lim_{n\to\infty} f(n) / g(n) = 0$.
\end{definition}

The key change from Big-O is from \emph{some} $c>0$ to
\emph{every} $c>0$. No matter how small $c$ is, 
$f(n)$ needs to stay below $c\cdot g(n)$ once $n \ge N_c$.
A smaller $c$ may require a larger $N_c$.
$f=o(g)$ means $f$ grows strictly slower than $g$, beyond any fixed factor;
small-o is analogous to ``$<$''.

%Equivalently, $f(n)/g(n)$ tends to zero, so $f$ becomes negligible compared with $g$.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(n) = n$ and $g(n) = n^2$. We now show that $f = o(g)$,
using the above definition.
To prove it, we need to show that for any possible $c>0$ there exists $N_c \ge 0$ such that $f(n) -c\cdot g(n) \le 0$ when $n \ge N_c$.
We write $f(n) - c\cdot g(n) = n - cn^2 = n(1-cn)$. Since $n\ge 0$, it suffices to require $1-cn \le 0$, leading to $n \ge 1/c$.
Therefore, we can choose $N_c = \max\{1, \lceil 1/c \rceil\}$.
When $n \ge N_c$, $cn \ge 1$, resulting in the desired $f(n) - c\cdot g(n) \le 0$.

%For example, $n<2n$ for every positive $n$, but $n$ is not
%$o(2n)$: their ratio is always $1/2$, so choosing $c=1/4$ makes the required
%inequality impossible. Instead, $n=\Theta(2n)$.

\begin{definition}[small-omega]
Let $f = f(n)$ and $g=g(n)$ be two functions that are positive for all integers $n$.
We say $f = \omega(g)$ if for every $c > 0$ there exists an integer $N_c \ge 0$, where $N_c$ may depend on $c$,
such that $f(n) \ge c\cdot g(n)$ for all $n\ge N_c$.
\end{definition}

Small-omega requires $f(n)$ to eventually exceed \emph{every} fixed
multiple of $g(n)$, however large that multiple is.
$f = \omega(g)$ means $f$ grows strictly faster than $g$, beyond any fixed factor;
small-omega is analogous to ``$>$''.
%A fixed factor is not enough;
%$2n$ is $\Omega(n)$ but is not $\omega(n)$.

\emph{Example.} We have $n^2=\omega(4n)$. To prove this, for any $c>0$, choose
$N_c=\max\{1,\lceil 4c\rceil\}$. Then $n\ge N_c$ implies $n\ge 4c$, so
$n^2 - c\cdot 4n = n(n-4c)\ge 0$.

\begin{claim}
$f = o(g)$ if and only if $g = \omega(f)$.
\end{claim}
\emph{Proof.} We have
\begin{displaymath}
\begin{array}{rll}
& f = o(g) \\
\Leftrightarrow & \forall c>0,\ \exists N_c\ge 0,\ \textrm{s.t.\ } f(n)\le c\cdot g(n),\ \forall n\ge N_c \\
\Leftrightarrow & \forall c>0,\ \exists N_c\ge 0,\ \textrm{s.t.\ } (1/c)\cdot f(n)\le g(n),\ \forall n\ge N_c \\
\Leftrightarrow & \forall c'>0,\ \exists N_{c'}\ge 0,\ \textrm{s.t.\ } g(n)\ge c'\cdot f(n),\ \forall n\ge N_{c'} \\
\Leftrightarrow & g = \omega(f).
\end{array}
\end{displaymath}
In the third equivalence, we set $c'=1/c$. As $c$ ranges over all positive
numbers, so does $c'$. The integer threshold 
$N_{c'}$ is simply $N_{1/c}$.  \qed


\begin{claim}
If $f = o(g)$ then $f = O(g)$; if $f = \omega(g)$ then $f = \Omega(g)$.
\end{claim}
This matches the intuition that ``$<$'' implies ``$\le$'' and ``$>$'' implies
``$\ge$''. However, this intuitive explanation does not constitute a proof.
To formally see this, choose $c=1$ in the definition of
small-o. Then the definition states that there exists a threshold $N_1$ such that $f(n)\le g(n)$ for all
$n\ge N_1$, which satisfies the definition of Big-O. The same argument,
with the inequality reversed, proves the second implication.

\subsection*{Comparing using Limits}
You might notice that the definitions above resemble the definitions of limits.
Indeed, if the ratio $f(n)/g(n)$ approaches a limit or tends to infinity,
we can infer an asymptotic relationship between $f$ and $g$.
We state the following facts without proof.
%measures the size of $f(n)$ relative to $g(n)$. If this ratio approaches zero,
%$f$ becomes negligible compared with $g$; if it approaches a positive constant,
%the functions differ by roughly a constant factor; if it tends to infinity,
%$f$ eventually exceeds every fixed multiple of $g$.
\begin{claim} \label{claim:limit}
\[
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=0
    &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f=o(g), \\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=c,\quad 0<c<\infty
    &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f=\Theta(g), \\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=\infty
    &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f=\omega(g).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{claim}

\emph{Example.} For $f(n)=n$ and $g(n)=n^2$, we have
\[
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n^2}
    =\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}=0.
\]
Thus $n=o(n^2)$, recovering our earlier result without choosing $N_c$ explicitly.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(n)=n^2$ and $g(n)=2^n$. To apply L'H\^opital's rule,
first consider the corresponding functions of a real variable $x$. Applying
the rule twice gives
\[
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2}{2^x}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x}{2^x\ln 2}
    =\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2}{2^x(\ln 2)^2}=0.
\]
The same limit holds along integer values $x=n$, so $n^2=o(2^n)$.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(n)=\log_b n$ and $g(n)=n$, where $b>1$ is a fixed
base. Using $\log_b x=\ln x/\ln b$ and applying L'H\^opital's rule once,
\[
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\log_b x}{x}
    =\frac{1}{\ln b}\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x}
    =\frac{1}{\ln b}\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{1}=0.
\]
Therefore $\log_b n=o(n)$.
%Although both functions grow without bound, the logarithm becomes an arbitrarily small fraction of $n$.

For any fixed $a,b>1$, the change-of-base formula gives
  \[ \log_b n = \log_b a \cdot \log_a n. \]
  Thus, logarithms with different fixed bases differ only by a constant factor.
  For this reason, we often write simply $\log n$ in asymptotic notation
  without specifying the base.

\emph{Example.} Let $f(n) = 2^n$ and $g(n) = 3^n$.
\[
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2^n}{3^n}
    =\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{(3/2)^n} = 0.
\]
Hence, $2^n = o(3^n)$.
Note that exponential bases matter in asymptotic comparisons.


\emph{Example.} Let $f(n)=(2+(-1)^n)n$ and $g(n)=n$ for $n\ge 1$.
Note that the ratio $f(n)/g(n)$ alternates between $1$ and $3$; in other words,
the ratio \emph{oscillates} without approaching a limit or infinity.
In this case, we cannot use Claim~\ref{claim:limit} to reach a conclusion.
We can still use the definition directly.
Note that $g(n)\le f(n)\le 3 g(n)$. Hence, $f=\Theta(g)$.
%Thus a positive finite limit implies Big-Theta, but Big-Theta does not require the ratio to have a limit.


\subsection*{Common Functions}

The following basic functions appear frequently as running-time in algorithm analysis.
%Here, logarithm bases are fixed constants greater than $1$, and exponents such as $k$ do not depend on $n$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item {Logarithmic function: $\log n$.}
%Examples: $\log\log n$, $\log n$, and $(\log n)^2$.
%Repeatedly halving a problem of size $n$ takes about $\log_2 n$ steps;
%this is why logarithms appear in binary search. A fixed power of a logarithm,
%such as $(\log n)^2$, is called polylogarithmic.
Logarithmic growth is slow: doubling $n$ adds only $1$ to $\log_2 n$.

\item {Linear function $n$ and power function $n^p$, for any fixed $p > 0$.}
%Examples: $\sqrt{n}=n^{0.5}$, $n$, $n\log n$, $n^{1.001}$, $n^2$, and $n^3$.
%A scan of $n$ elements takes linear time, while examining all pairs often
%takes quadratic time. Merge sort has a $\Theta(n\log n)$ running time,
%combining about $\log n$ levels of recursion with linear work per level.
%Strictly speaking, $n\log n$ is not a polynomial, but it is bounded above
%by one; for example, $n\log n=O(n^2)$. For a fixed power $n^k$, doubling
%$n$ multiplies the function by $2^k$.

\item {Exponential function: $a^n$ for any fixed $a > 1$.}
%Examples: $2^n$, $n2^n$, and $3^n$.
%A set of $n$ elements has $2^n$ subsets, so examining every subset naturally
%leads to an exponential bound. If processing each subset takes linear time,
%the resulting bound is $O(n2^n)$.
Exponential functions grow fast: increasing $n$ by $1$ multiplies $a^n$ by a constant factor $a$.

\item {Factorial function: $n!=1\cdot 2\cdots n$.}
%There are $n!$ permutations of $n$ distinct elements, so factorial bounds
%arise when enumerating all possible orderings.
Increasing $n$ to $n+1$ multiplies $n!$ by $n+1$;
this growing multiplier makes factorial growth
faster than exponential functions.
\end{enumerate}

These functions form a hierarchy under the small-o relation: for any fixed $a>1$,
we have
\[
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n}{n}=0, \qquad
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{a^n}=0, \qquad
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a^n}{n!}=0.
\]
Hence,
\[
    \log n=o(n),\qquad
    n=o(a^n),\qquad
    a^n=o(n!).
\]

Moreover, any fixed positive power of one of these functions grows strictly
slower than any fixed positive power of the next, however large the
first power or small the second power is. Formally, 
\begin{claim}\label{claim:growth}
For any fixed constants $a>1$, $p>0$, and $\epsilon>0$, we have
\[
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\log n)^p}{n^\epsilon}=0, \qquad
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^p}{a^{\epsilon n}}=0, \qquad
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a^{pn}}{(n!)^\epsilon}=0.
\]
Hence,
\[
    (\log n)^p =o(n^\epsilon),\qquad
    n^p =o(a^{\epsilon n}),\qquad
    a^{pn} = o((n!)^\epsilon).
\]
\end{claim}

These basic functions can be combined using sums, products, and composition.
The following examples show how to compare them.

\emph{Example (a sum of terms).} Let $f(n)=3n^2+5n+20$.
The quadratic term \emph{dominates}, as it grows
at least as fast as every other term.
Hence, $f = \Theta(n^2)$.


\emph{Example.}
Compare $n (\log n)^3$ and $n^{1.01}$. Using Claim~\ref{claim:growth},
we have
\[
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n (\log n)^3}{n^{1.01}}
    = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\log n)^3}{n^{0.01}} = 0
.
\]
Hence $n (\log n)^3 = o(n^{1.01})$.


\emph{Example.}
Compare $n^2 2^n$ and $3^n$. Using Claim~\ref{claim:growth},
\[
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2 2^n}{3^n}
    =\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2}{(3/2)^n}=0.
\]
Hence $n^2 2^n = o(3^n)$.

\emph{Example.} Compare $\log(\log n)$ and $\log n$.
%where all logarithms have the same fixed base $b>1$.
Set $s=\log n$.
As $n\to\infty$, we also have $s\to\infty$, so
\[
    \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(\log n)}{\log n}
    =\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{\log s}{s}=0.
\]
%The last equality is the logarithm-versus-linear comparison proved earlier.
Hence $\log(\log n) =o(\log n)$.

\emph{Example.} Compare $\log(n!)$ and
$n\log n$. The logarithm turns the product defining $n!$ into a sum:
\[
    \log(n!)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log k.
\]
Each term is at most $\log n$, giving $\log(n!)\le n\log n$.
Hence, $\log(n!) = O(n\log n)$.
For a lower bound, note that at least $n/2$ of the integers from $1$ to $n$ are
at least $n/2$. Keeping just these terms gives, for $n\ge 4$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \log(n!) &\ge \sum_{k=\lceil n/2\rceil}^n \log k
    \ge \frac{n}{2}\log\frac{n}{2} \\
    &=\frac{n}{2}(\log n-\log 2)
    \ge \frac{n}{4}\log n.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last inequality uses $\log n\ge 2\log 2$ for $n\ge 4$.
This gives $\log(n!)=\Omega(n\log n)$.
Combined, $\log(n!)=\Theta(n\log n)$.
